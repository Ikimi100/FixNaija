The presidential message

Government will be judged by what changes in the lives of Nigerians.

— Prince (Dr.) Adewole Adebayo, Esq. · p.ix

Nigeria does not suffer from a shortage of human talent, natural resources or patriotic aspiration. The central challenge, the blueprint says, is to organise the State so that these assets become security, productive opportunity and a dignified life for every citizen.

By 2031 Nigerians should be safer in their homes, farms, schools, markets and on the roads; food and energy should be more affordable; power and transport more reliable; young people should have real paths into skills, enterprise and decent work; and public institutions should be more transparent and accountable.

“This is not a catalogue of aspirations. It is a programme for delivery.” Ministers and agencies get measurable targets and deadlines, performance is reported publicly, and failing programmes are corrected.

How the government will think

The governing philosophy

Four ideas sit underneath every programme in the blueprint. Together they reject “the false choice between an active State and a productive market” — Nigeria needs both.

📜

Chapter II as the compass

The Constitution's Fundamental Objectives become an operating mandate: every major policy must name the objective it serves and report measurable results. pp.1–6

🤝

Social democracy

Markets and private enterprise drive growth; the State steps in strategically where markets fail, where essential public goods are at stake, or where catalytic investment is needed. p.1

🍲

The Basic Needs Approach

Security, food, clean water, affordable energy, healthcare, education, housing, decent work, mobility, justice and digital access come first. p.1

🧩

Cooperative federalism

Federal, State and Local Governments act as development partners, with each job done by the lowest level of government able to do it well. pp.1–2

Twelve presidential missions

The plan, sector by sector

What the blueprint will do

🛡️Part III · Defence & internal securityOne Republic, one security systemPolice rebuilt as the main internal-security force, intelligence that warns early, and no community left to criminals.+
  • A single national security doctrine: the Police rebuilt as the principal institution for internal security, the Armed Forces focused on external defence and exceptional internal operations, intelligence focused on early warning. pp.32–33
  • The President chairs a strengthened National Security Council; a National Joint Intelligence and Operations Platform links military, police, intelligence, border, financial and emergency agencies, with joint fusion centres in every major theatre. p.33
  • A National Anti-Kidnapping and Banditry Command: every reported kidnapping gets a unified case file, a victim-recovery plan and a financial-intelligence investigation — plus mobile police units, forest patrols, aerial surveillance and secured farm corridors. p.35
  • Constitutionally grounded state policing with strong safeguards against abuse, national minimum standards and independent state police service commissions. p.36
  • Police transformation: competitive, geographically balanced recruitment; better pay, insurance, housing, pensions and medical care; stations with transparent budgets, vehicles, forensic kits and digital case files. p.35
  • A defence industry that makes ammunition, body armour, secure radios, drones, patrol boats and tactical vehicles at home, backed by a Defence Innovation Fund. p.34
  • Oil theft treated as “organised economic warfare” — follow the money and prosecute senior beneficiaries. p.37
  • Security plans for schools, markets, motor parks and farming seasons; ghost workers, fuel diversion and ammunition theft prosecuted, with savings sent to frontline welfare. pp.40–41
“Security is the first duty of the state and the indispensable foundation of economic transformation, social justice and national unity.” p.32
🏭Part II · Economic transformationFrom importing to producingA self-reliant economy that works for every citizen by 2031, built on import substitution and regional industrial clusters.+
  • The vision: “a self-reliant Nigerian economy that works for every citizen by 2031” — reducing import dependence dramatically within four years. p.8
  • Import substitution aimed at what Nigeria actually imports most — manufactured goods (about 44% of imports, with motor vehicles a major part) and petroleum products — not just food (about 6%). p.8
  • Auto industrialisation: federal procurement priority for Nigerian-made and Nigerian-assembled vehicles; foreign makers encouraged — and, where legally permissible, required — to assemble in Nigeria. p.9
  • Industrial clusters and parks in every geopolitical zone for agro-processing, textiles, pharmaceuticals, vehicle assembly, electronics and building materials. p.12
  • Complete the steel projects at Ajaokuta, Jos and Katsina, and reposition the Ikot Abasi aluminium smelter and the Osogbo Machine Tools Company. p.24
  • Exports of processed foods, textiles, pharmaceuticals, leather and value-added minerals, aiming to become a net exporter within the AfCFTA. pp.14–15
  • An SME Development Fund and guarantee schemes; simple registration and a path to formality for informal businesses. pp.12, 16
  • Borrow for productive investment, not recurrent spending; broaden the tax base and cut multiple taxation without burdening small businesses. p.17
“We are not promising miracles. We are promising a clear direction, consistent implementation, and measurable results.” p.31
🌾Mission 2 · Feed NigeriaFood that is grown, processed and sold at homeSeeds, irrigation, mechanisation and processing hubs — and fewer imported staples.+
  • Improved seeds, fertiliser, irrigation and mechanisation; farm-estate clusters and out-grower schemes with inputs, credit and extension services for smallholders. p.10
  • Agro-processing hubs in every geopolitical zone. p.10
  • Within four years, substantially reduce or eliminate imports of rice, wheat, sugar, dairy and poultry, and build strategic food reserves. p.11
  • Grow exports of cocoa, sesame, cashew and ginger. p.11
  • Land, finance and markets for young people and women in agribusiness; rural roads, power, water and storage; climate-smart farming. p.11
  • Year one: reform input delivery, expand irrigation and launch youth agribusiness programmes. p.11
  • Protection — and compensation — so farmers can return to their land, and seasonal security plans for farming zones. pp.38, 41
⚡Missions 3 & 4 · Power & affordable energy75,000 MW and cheaper fuelDon't wait for the national grid alone — let states, communities and businesses generate power, and refine fuel at home.+
  • Target: 75,000 megawatts generated and distributed by all means possible within four years, 2027–2031. p.20
  • A National Distributed Electricity Initiative: stand-alone power for states, LGAs, communities, cooperatives, schools, hospitals, markets and households — solar, small hydro, wind, biomass, gas or hybrids — with simple approvals and accredited installers. p.20
  • A Bank of Electricity giving long-term, single-digit financing for mini-grids and distributed generation, and for local manufacture of transformers, meters, solar panels, inverters and batteries. p.20
  • “Consumers will not be compelled to pay for electricity that is not supplied.” Faster metering, lower losses, and households allowed to sell surplus power where feasible. p.21
  • Fuel: transparent crude supply to domestic refineries and, within the first eighteen months, measurable increases in local output of petrol, diesel and Jet A-1. pp.13–14
  • Refineries that receive concessions must pass efficiency gains to consumers; regulators publish price data and act against collusion and artificial scarcity. pp.13–14
  • Gas gathering, pipelines, LPG and CNG, with gas-flaring reduction treated as a development priority. p.14
🚆Mission 8 · Modern infrastructureRoads, rail, aviation and ports that move the economyMetro rail for 21 priority cities, aircraft maintenance hubs, river ports and highways linking every zone.+
  • Federal highways linking all zones, state capitals, industrial clusters, mining areas, ports and border corridors. p.21
  • Aviation: cheaper fares through domestic jet-fuel refining and streamlined charges; aircraft Maintenance, Repair and Overhaul hubs in Lagos, Abuja, Kano and Port Harcourt (2027–2028), then Enugu, Kaduna and Maiduguri or Gombe (2028–2031), each with a training academy. pp.21–22
  • A Nigerian aircraft manufacturing and assembly company, beginning assembly of regional and utility aircraft within the first term — and over 15,000 direct high-skilled jobs. pp.22–23
  • A National Metro Rail Development Programme giving priority to 21 cities — Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, Kano, Ibadan, Kaduna, Aba, Maiduguri, Uyo, Warri, Katsina, Benin City, Enugu, Gusau, Onitsha, Jos, Abakaliki, Kebbi, Yola, Akure and Abeokuta — with local production of coaches, locomotives and track. pp.23–24
  • Upgraded ports and jetties, and the Niger and Benue dredged for river ports at Onitsha, Idah, Makurdi and Yola. pp.24–25
  • Broadband and 5G, irrigation, water and sanitation — and hundreds of thousands of construction jobs by 2031. p.25
“Air travel must no longer be a luxury reserved for the privileged few.” p.21
💼Mission 5 · Jobs and enterpriseWork for every willing NigerianEmployment becomes the central test of economic policy, with an employment guarantee and a national skills revolution.+
  • Productive employment made “the central test of economic policy”; every major policy carries explicit job-creation targets. pp.x, 16
  • A National Employment and Livelihood Strategy aimed at creating millions of sustainable jobs within four years. p.78
  • A National Employment Guarantee Programme: every Nigerian willing and able to work but unable to find a job gets access to temporary, productive work — infrastructure maintenance, afforestation, flood control, sanitation, rural roads — with training and a route into private jobs or enterprise. p.79
  • A National Employment and Skills Council, chaired by the Vice-President, setting annual national employment targets. p.79
  • A National Skills Revolution Initiative for millions of young Nigerians, modernised technical colleges, regional Centres of Technical Excellence and a certified apprenticeship system. pp.79–80
  • A National Enterprise Incubation Network across the six geopolitical zones, with start-up finance, mentorship and simpler business registration. p.80
  • Big infrastructure contracts carry jobs, apprenticeship and technology-transfer obligations as measurable contract deliverables. p.81
“The most effective and dignified form of social welfare is productive employment.” p.78
🤲Part V · Social welfare & inclusionEnding extreme povertyA Presidency-led poverty strategy, help that arrives in days, and digital payments that cannot be diverted.+
  • “The eradication of extreme poverty is the foremost social objective of this administration” — a National Poverty Elimination Strategy supervised directly by the Presidency. p.55
  • Target: reduce multidimensional poverty by at least fifty percent during the first term. Every ministry sets annual poverty-reduction targets. p.56
  • Rural Enterprise Growth Zones in every senatorial district — irrigation, storage, agro-processing, roads, electricity, broadband and finance. p.57
  • A National Household Enterprise Programme: training, low-interest credit, cooperatives and market links for micro-businesses. p.56
  • A Universal Social Protection Framework and a National Social Protection Authority created by Act of the National Assembly, with emergency help that arrives “within days rather than months”. pp.59–60
  • Universal health insurance coverage for poor households. p.57
  • One national social registry, a digital account for every beneficiary, a 24-hour operations centre and a tracking number for every complaint. pp.87–88
  • Paid for mainly through wealth creation — “rather than excessive borrowing or distortionary taxation” — and by cutting waste and duplicated agencies. pp.90–91
“Every economic policy shall become a poverty policy.” p.56
🏠Section 5.7 · Housing & urban renewalThree million affordable homesCheaper mortgages, digital land titles, satellite cities — and no indiscriminate demolition.+
  • A National Affordable Housing and Sustainable Communities Programme to facilitate at least three million affordable housing units within four years, through public investment, PPPs, cooperatives and private builders. p.75
  • Mortgage reform — progressively lower rates, longer repayment periods, simpler paperwork — plus a reformed Federal Mortgage Bank and a National Housing Fund open to informal-sector workers. p.75
  • Digital land registries and simpler title registration, working with the states. pp.75–76
  • No indiscriminate demolition: upgrade slums first; where relocation can't be avoided, consult residents and give fair compensation and suitable alternative housing. p.76
  • Satellite cities around Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, Kano, Ibadan, Kaduna, Enugu, Benin City and Maiduguri, and a Rural Housing Improvement Programme. p.76
  • Hundreds of thousands of young Nigerians trained in construction trades; housing schemes for public servants, soldiers, police, health workers and teachers; shared-equity first homes for youth. pp.76–77
👪Sections 5.4–5.5 · Women, children & inclusionHalf the cabinet for womenSafer motherhood, protected children, and a country where disability does not decide destiny.+
  • Women to hold not less than 50 percent of cabinet-level appointments, and of appointments from boards to ambassadors; incentives for a private-sector “50 percent inclusion policy”. p.63
  • A National Women's Economic Empowerment Programme with special financing windows for women-owned businesses and women's cooperatives. pp.63–64
  • Maternal health as a national priority: upgraded primary health centres, trained community health workers, blood banks and emergency transport. p.64
  • Early Childhood Development centres, school feeding with local produce, and a child protection unit in every state. pp.64–65
  • A National Council on Women, Children and Family Development, chaired by the Vice-President. p.66
  • Persons with disabilities: accessibility plans in every federal agency; all new federal buildings, terminals and digital platforms fully accessible; a National Assistive Technology Programme. pp.67–68
  • Senior citizens: geriatric clinics in primary healthcare, community centres and income support for indigent older people. p.69
“Children shall never be criminalised merely because of homelessness or poverty.” p.65
🎓Part VII · Education & researchLearning that leads to workFree basic education, hands-on skills from secondary school, and respect for technical training.+
  • Universal Basic Education will be free, in line with Chapter II of the Constitution, and the number of out-of-school children significantly reduced. pp.104, 106
  • Hands-on vocational and entrepreneurial training from secondary school, matched to local economies — mining technology in mining areas, agro-processing in farming areas, aquaculture in riverine areas. p.104
  • Integrated school farms in rural public schools, supplying school feeding programmes. p.104
  • Technical and vocational education given equal recognition with university education, with modernised technical colleges and national certification. pp.104–105
  • More research funding, and a Consultancy Services Directorate in every federal tertiary institution. pp.105–106
  • Better recruitment, training and welfare for teachers; smart classrooms, digital libraries and responsible use of AI. pp.105–106
“No education system can rise above the quality of its teachers.” p.105
🏥Part VIII · HealthcarePrevention first — and a war on malariaWorking primary care, wider health insurance, doctors who stay, and medicines made at home.+
  • Primary health centres rehabilitated, staffed and equipped; Universal Health Insurance expanded, with special protection for children, pregnant women, older people and persons with disabilities. p.107
  • A National Malaria Eradication, Vaccine Industrialisation and Mass Immunisation Programme: an estimated US$4.5 billion (about ₦8.1 trillion) over four years, targeting an 80% cut in malaria cases and a 90% cut in malaria deaths by 2031. pp.107–108
  • Malaria vaccines made in Nigeria through technology transfer; the programme's vaccines, diagnostics, nets, medicines and logistics are targeted to create not fewer than 500,000 direct jobs and more than a million indirect jobs. pp.107–108
  • Guaranteed housemanship for every eligible medical graduate, automatic absorption of qualified resident doctors into residency, better pay to cut brain drain, and rural service incentives. pp.108, 110
  • Pharmaceutical self-sufficiency, and scientific research and standards for traditional and herbal medicine. pp.108–110
  • Electronic medical records, telemedicine, ambulance networks and trauma centres. p.109
“Nigeria's healthcare system can only be as strong as the professionals who sustain it.” p.108
💡Part IX · Technology, AI & the digital economyFrom consumer to producer of technologyA national AI strategy, innovation hubs in every zone and affordable broadband everywhere.+
  • A National AI Strategy for education, health, agriculture, transport, security, tax and justice — with an ethical framework that protects privacy and citizens' rights. p.111
  • A National Innovation Fund, and innovation hubs and technology parks across all six geopolitical zones. p.111
  • Affordable broadband nationwide; grants, venture finance and simpler regulation for start-ups. p.111
  • Public procurement preference, where appropriate, for competitively priced Nigerian technology. p.112
  • Satellite and space applications, and government services progressively fully digital, protected by a national cybersecurity architecture. p.112
⚖️Part X · Governance, justice & constitutional reformJustice in twelve months, open books in every LGAFaster courts, independent anti-graft agencies, electoral reform and transparent local governments.+
  • Courts: final determination of cases within twelve months, save for genuinely exceptional matters — through e-filing, case management, limits on adjournments and virtual hearings. pp.113–114
  • Prosecutions guided by law and published public-interest principles, not partisan direction — and a review of separating the offices of Minister of Justice and Attorney-General. p.114
  • Implement essential recommendations of the Justice Mohammed Uwais Electoral Reform Committee Report. p.114
  • Truly independent EFCC, ICPC and other anti-corruption agencies with security of tenure; no officer may lawfully direct them to start, end or suppress a case for partisan purposes. pp.114–115
  • All 774 Local Governments publish quarterly reports of every revenue received and every expenditure, on a public digital platform. p.116
  • A Minister of Inter-Governmental Affairs, and a merit-based public service with annual performance contracts. pp.113, 115
“No Nigerian shall be above the law, and no Nigerian shall be targeted through the law for political reasons.” p.115
🌍Part IV · Foreign policyDiplomacy that delivers at homeAfrica first, a rebuilt ECOWAS, trade that creates jobs, and a 24-hour line for Nigerians in trouble abroad.+
  • Africa as the first circle of foreign policy; rebuild trust in ECOWAS and defend constitutional order against coups. p.45
  • Embassies as delivery platforms with annual targets, and Nigeria Trade and Investment Desks in major commercial centres. p.45
  • The AfCFTA used as an instrument of industrial growth, not a route for uncontrolled imports. p.47
  • A 24-hour Global Nigerian Consular Service with a single emergency line, and an end to passport scarcity, unofficial charges and delays. p.48
  • A Global Nigerian Skills and Enterprise Register and diaspora investment instruments; diaspora voting pursued with safeguards. p.49
  • Diversified partnerships — Nigeria “will not become an appendage of any bloc.” p.47
🚨Sections 5.6 & 5.9 · Disasters & national servicePrepared before disaster, serving afterA stronger NEMA, flood prevention, and a national corps of volunteers in every LGA.+
  • NEMA reformed into Nigeria's premier disaster-risk and resilience agency, with disaster management committees in every Local Government. p.71
  • A National Flood Prevention and Mitigation Programme and humanitarian logistics centres across the geopolitical zones. p.72
  • A National Disaster Resilience Fund that reports every year to the National Assembly. p.74
  • A National Volunteer and Community Service Corps alongside the NYSC, with Community Service Centres in every LGA. pp.82, 85
⚽Part VI · Sports developmentTalent from every school fieldSchool sports restored nationwide and a Presidential Schools Football Cup that builds stadiums.+
  • Organised sports restored from secondary schools through universities, polytechnics and colleges of education. p.102
  • A Presidential Schools Football Cup — LGA, state, zonal and national rounds — with a modern football stadium for the champion school. p.102
  • Thousands of coaches and technical officials trained and certified, and new regional academies. pp.102–103
  • A reformed Nigerian Football League — transparent governance, club licensing, TV broadcasting — aiming to be Africa's premier competition. p.103
The four-year delivery framework

How it gets done

A clear sequence from day one, and machinery that forces results rather than announcements.

Day 100

Set up to deliver

A Presidential Delivery and Performance Office, baseline audits of security, power, food, health, education and infrastructure, Federal–State delivery compacts and the first public delivery dashboard.

Year 1

Stabilise and mobilise

Restore security in priority corridors, speed up domestic refining, expand farm production, start power and transport quick wins, strengthen primary healthcare and mobilise youth skills and jobs.

Years 2–3

Build and scale

Industrial clusters, distributed power, irrigation and storage, pharmaceutical and tech manufacturing, rail and urban transport, broadband, housing and water — and successful State partnerships taken national.

Year 4

Consolidate and account

Finish priority projects, lock successful programmes into lasting institutions, publish audited outcome reports and present Nigerians with an end-of-term scorecard.

🏛️Delivery Office

A compact Presidential Delivery and Performance Office tracks the flagship missions, clears bottlenecks and reports directly to the President.

📝Performance contracts

Ministers and heads of key agencies sign annual contracts with a few measurable outcomes — not long lists of activities.

📊Quarterly public dashboard

Milestones, delays, spending and corrective action published every quarter in a form citizens can read.

📜Annual Chapter II review

A yearly report showing how major programmes and spending advance the Constitution's Fundamental Objectives.

🔁Evaluate and correct

Failing programmes are redesigned, merged or stopped — not kept alive for political convenience.

💰Spend on what produces

Less waste, borrowing directed at infrastructure and productive investment, and stronger non-oil revenue without punitive taxation.

Implementation principle

“No major policy shall be treated as complete merely because it has been announced, funded or launched.” p.xi

Measured in 2031

The national scorecard

The headline outcomes the administration says it will publish a final scorecard against.

SecuritySafer communities, farms, schools, markets and transport corridors; faster intelligence-led response to threats.
FoodHigher domestic output, lower post-harvest losses and more affordable staple foods.
PowerMore reliable electricity and rapid growth of distributed and embedded generation.
Energy costMore domestic refining and gas use, with fewer logistics and supply bottlenecks.
JobsLarge-scale growth in productive employment, apprenticeships, enterprise and industrial work.
PovertyA sustained reduction in extreme poverty through jobs, productivity and targeted social protection.
HealthStronger primary care, wider insurance cover, better workforce retention and more domestic pharmaceutical capacity.
EducationBetter basic learning, more technical skills and stronger research–industry links.
InfrastructureVisible improvement in roads, rail, aviation, water, housing, broadband and urban mobility.
TechnologyWider digital public services, stronger AI and innovation capacity, more indigenous technology.
GovernanceRoutine publication of spending and performance, stronger procurement integrity and faster public services.
Federal partnershipMore jointly financed, performance-based Federal–State–Local programmes.
Foreign policyDiplomacy visibly linked to trade, investment, technology, security cooperation and protection of Nigerians abroad.
Public trustGovernment judged by accessible evidence of delivery rather than announcements.

Detailed baselines and annual targets are to be set after a transition-period audit, so that targets rest on verified national data. p.xiv
